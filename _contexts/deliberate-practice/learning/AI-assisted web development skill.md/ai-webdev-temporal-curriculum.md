#### Stack: Next.js 15 · Tailwind · Sanity CMS · TypeScript
#### Companion to: The Seven-Theme Skill Curriculum (v3.0)

## Preface: Why Time Is Its Own Curriculum

The Seven-Theme Curriculum addresses how work goes wrong in quality and direction. This curriculum addresses how work goes wrong in time. These are different failure modes with different causes and different corrections.

The central finding of fifty years of research on software project failure — from Kahneman and Tversky's 1979 Planning Fallacy research through the Standish Group's Chaos Reports through 2026 industry data — is this: **developers are not bad at building. They are systematically, reliably, predictably bad at knowing how long building will take.** Studies consistently show developers underestimate task duration by 25-75%. Complex features routinely take 2-3x the initial estimate. This is not a character flaw. It is a cognitive architecture flaw — the human brain is structurally poor at prospective time estimation.

The six themes in this curriculum address the six distinct temporal failure modes:

```
Theme T1 — Estimation Accuracy     Why your initial estimate is always wrong,
                                    and how to make it less wrong

Theme T2 — Time Visibility          Why you cannot feel time passing accurately,
                                    and how to make time visible in real-time

Theme T3 — Velocity Monitoring      How to know whether you are on track,
                                    before it is too late to correct

Theme T4 — Scope-Time Calibration   The relationship between scope and time,
                                    and the discipline of cutting scope to save deadline

Theme T5 — Recovery Protocols       What to do when you are behind —
                                    specifically, in order, without panic

Theme T6 — Time Psychology          The cognitive and emotional failures that
                                    destroy time discipline, and how to counter them
```

## The Core Temporal Failure of Sang-Logium

Before the themes: a precise reconstruction of the time failure.

**The original plan:** Complete homepage by March 5. 6 days of work.
**The actual result:** 17 days. Homepage incomplete.
**The gap:** 11 days unaccounted for.

The question this curriculum answers is: what temporal skills, if present, would have made this impossible?

**The answer requires distinguishing two separate time failures:**

*Failure 1 — Estimation failure:* The original 6-day estimate was wrong. A 9-component homepage with real data integration on an unfamiliar new design was not a 6-day job for this developer at this skill level. The estimate was generated from the inside view — imagining the best-case build sequence — without reference to actual historical data or a realistic accounting of unknown complexity.

*Failure 2 — Velocity monitoring failure:* By March 4 (day 5), the developer recognized they were 10x behind schedule. That recognition came too late to course-correct meaningfully, and even when it arrived, it produced crushing demoralization rather than a structured recovery protocol. The time signals were not being read until the gap was catastrophic.

Both failures are addressable. This curriculum addresses them systematically.

## Theme T1: Estimation Accuracy

### The Mental Representation Being Built

> "My initial estimate for any task is a first draft, not a commitment. It is generated by my optimistic imagination, not by data. Before I act on it, I must stress-test it against three questions: What does history say? What could go wrong? What do I not know yet?"

### What Estimation Accuracy Is

Estimation accuracy is the skill of generating time estimates that are close enough to reality to be useful for planning — not perfect, but calibrated. A calibrated estimate is honest about its uncertainty, accounts for historical data, and includes explicit buffers for the unknown.

It is not the skill of being right. No developer is reliably right about estimates. It is the skill of being **less wrong in a predictable direction** — of knowing when your estimate is likely to be too optimistic and correcting for that bias before committing to a deadline.

### First Principle: The Planning Fallacy Is Not Personal Failure

The Planning Fallacy was identified by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in 1979. It is a cognitive bias in which people underestimate the time needed to complete tasks, even when they know from past experience that similar tasks took longer. It is universal across cultures, professions, and experience levels. Conscientious people fall for it as reliably as careless ones. It is not fixable by trying harder or caring more.

The mechanism: when you estimate a task, your brain generates an **inside view** — a mental simulation of the task going smoothly, proceeding through each step in sequence, with no interruptions, no bugs, no unexpected complexity. This simulation produces an optimistic number. It produces it every time, for everyone.

The correction is not willpower. It is a **procedural intervention**: deliberately taking the outside view by consulting data from similar past situations before finalizing the estimate.

**Research data:**
- Developers underestimate tasks by 25-75% on average
- Complex features routinely take 2-3x the initial estimate
- Only 30% of students in one study finished their thesis within their self-predicted time
- The Standish Group found 71% of US IT projects exceed time and budget constraints
- These numbers do not improve with experience unless the developer actively corrects for the bias

The sang-logium estimate of "complete homepage in 6 days" was generated entirely from the inside view. No reference to comparable past work. No accounting for the new design system. No buffer for unknown complexity. No acknowledgment that similar tasks had taken longer before.

### Sub-Skill T1.1: The Outside View — Reference Class Estimation

The outside view asks: "When people have done tasks similar to this one in the past, how long did those tasks actually take?"

For a solo developer, the reference class is your own history. You do not need a database of other developers. You need honest memory of how long your own comparable tasks took — not your best performance, but your average and your worst.

**The three questions of the outside view:**

```
1. REFERENCE CLASS: What is the closest category of work I have done before
   that resembles this task?

2. HISTORICAL DURATION: When I have done work in that reference class,
   what was the actual time range? (Minimum, average, maximum)

3. ADJUSTMENT: How does this specific task compare to the reference class?
   What makes it harder? What makes it easier?
```

**Applied to the sang-logium homepage estimate:**

```
REFERENCE CLASS: "Building a multi-section landing page with CMS data
and a new visual design system"

HISTORICAL DURATION: 
  Minimum (best case, everything worked): ~8 days
  Average (realistic, a few bugs, some uncertainty): ~14 days
  Maximum (design uncertainty, new patterns, regressions): ~21 days

ADJUSTMENT — factors making this harder than average:
  + New design language (not iterating on existing design)
  + Carousel component with mobile requirements (unknown complexity)
  + Sanity CMS integration (potential schema mismatches)
  + 9 distinct sections (more than typical landing page)

ADJUSTED ESTIMATE: 14-21 days, most likely 16-18 days

HONEST DEADLINE: March 15 (starting Feb 27), with March 10 as
"amber alert" date if fewer than 5 components are locked
```

The developer estimated 6 days. The outside view would have produced 14-21 days. The inside view was off by a factor of 2.5-3.5x — within the exact range that research predicts.

**Violation consequence — sang-logium:**
The 6-day estimate created a deadline (March 5) that was structurally impossible to meet. This means the developer was in a position of certain failure from day 1, without knowing it. The crushing demoralization of March 4 ("I realized I was 10x behind") was not a discovery — it was a delayed recognition of a fact that was true on day 1. Correct estimation would not have fixed the work, but it would have calibrated expectations realistically, allowed for early decision-making about scope, and prevented the psychological collapse that comes from discovering the gap late.

### Sub-Skill T1.2: The Uncertainty Multiplier

Every estimate contains hidden unknowns — things you do not know you do not know. Rumsfeld's "unknown unknowns." In software, these are: bugs in dependencies, APIs behaving differently than documented, browser compatibility issues, unexpected Sanity schema constraints, Tailwind class conflicts, Next.js version-specific behavior.

The correct response to unknown unknowns is not to enumerate them (you cannot enumerate what you don't know). It is to apply a multiplier to your estimate that accounts for the statistical likelihood of encountering them.

**The multiplier table (from research and industry practice):**

```
Task type                              Multiplier to apply to inside-view estimate
----------------------------------------------------------------------
Familiar task, familiar technology     1.25x — 25% buffer
Familiar task, unfamiliar technology   1.5x — 50% buffer
Unfamiliar task, familiar technology   1.75x — 75% buffer
Unfamiliar task, unfamiliar technology 2.0-2.5x — 100-150% buffer
New design system + new components     2.0x minimum
```

**Sang-logium category:** New design system (unfamiliar task) + Sanity CMS integration (partially familiar technology) + carousel component (unfamiliar implementation). This is solidly in the 1.75-2.0x range. An inside-view estimate of 6 days multiplied by 1.75 gives 10.5 days. Multiplied by 2.0 gives 12 days. Both are closer to reality than 6 days, though still optimistic given the 17-day actual outcome.

**The rule:** Never commit to a deadline based on an inside-view estimate alone. Always apply the multiplier. Tell yourself (and anyone else) the multiplied number, not the inside-view number.

### Sub-Skill T1.3: Task Decomposition Before Estimating

The Planning Fallacy is reduced when tasks are broken into concrete, small subtasks before estimating. This is called the **segmentation effect** — smaller chunks are harder to over-optimize mentally because there is less room to hand-wave complexity.

**The wrong way to estimate the sang-logium homepage:**
"Build homepage — 6 days"

**The right way:**
```
Pre-flight (scope contracts + DoD checklists for all 9 components): 2 hours
Pass 1 — Skeleton pass (all 9 components as empty boxes):           1-2 hours
Pass 2 — Data pass (all 9 components with Sanity data):             3-6 hours
Pass 3 — Build pass, per component:
  Hero (existing, mostly done):                                     1-2 hours
  Shelf (structural wrapper):                                       30 min
  RedesignFeatured+Spotlight (carousel, complex):                   6-10 hours
  ProductSpotlight2:                                                2-3 hours
  ProductSpotlight3:                                                2-3 hours
  IemsGallery:                                                      3-4 hours
  NewestRelease:                                                     3-4 hours
  Dacs:                                                             2-3 hours
  Accessories:                                                      2-3 hours
Mobile DoD verification for each component:                         1-2 hours each
Integration bugs and cross-component issues:                        4-8 hours buffer
Total range: 35-55 hours = 6-9 working days (6 hour days)
```

This decomposed estimate is still optimistic (17 days happened), but it is visibly more complex than "6 days" and immediately surfaces the carousel as a high-uncertainty item deserving extra buffer.

**The rule:** Before estimating any multi-component project, list every component and every pass. Estimate each line item separately. Sum them. Add 20% for integration. That is your estimate. Never estimate the whole without decomposing first.

### Sub-Skill T1.4: The "Amber Alert" Date

Every project with a deadline needs two dates, not one:

```
DEADLINE DATE: The date the deliverable must be complete.
AMBER ALERT DATE: The date by which, if you are not at a specific milestone,
                  you know with certainty that you will miss the deadline.
```

The Amber Alert date exists to trigger early course-correction, not late-stage crisis management. It is calculated backwards from the deadline based on your velocity estimate.

**Sang-logium example:**
```
DEADLINE: March 5
AMBER ALERT DATE: March 2 (day 3 of 6)
AMBER ALERT CONDITION: If fewer than 3 components are locked by March 2,
                       the deadline is unreachable at current velocity.
AMBER ALERT RESPONSE: Immediately decide to either (a) cut scope,
                       (b) extend deadline, or (c) increase daily hours.
```

Without an Amber Alert date, the developer continued building until March 4 (day 5 of 6) before recognizing the gap. By then, the response options had collapsed — there was almost no time for any meaningful correction. The Amber Alert date would have surfaced the crisis on day 3, with 3 days remaining to respond.

**Violation consequence — sang-logium:**
The recognition of being "10x behind" on March 4 came with only 1 day remaining before the original deadline. At that point, the emotional impact was crushing and the practical options were gone. An Amber Alert on March 2 would have produced: "I am behind. I have 3 days. My options are: (a) cut 4 components from the scope and ship 5, (b) push the deadline by 5 days, (c) put in 10-hour days for 3 days." All of those are manageable. Discovering the problem on March 4 produced: "I have failed." Nothing manageable.

## Theme T2: Time Visibility

### The Mental Representation Being Built

> "I cannot feel time passing accurately while I am building. The sense of time that I feel while coding is not calibrated to the clock. I need external instruments to see time, the same way I need a speedometer to know how fast I am driving — I cannot reliably feel it."

### What Time Visibility Is

Time visibility is the practice of making the passage of time externally observable during work — so that you know when an hour has passed, when you have spent 15 minutes on a bug, when a session has lasted 3 hours — without having to rely on your felt sense of time, which is unreliable.

In flow states (common during focused development work), time passes rapidly and invisibly. An hour feels like 15 minutes. Three hours disappear. This is not a problem if the work is directed correctly. It is catastrophic if the work is directed incorrectly — because the time lost to the perfectionism loop or the over-engineering spiral is not felt until it is already gone.

### First Principle: Parkinson's Law and the Absence of Time Pressure

Parkinson's Law states: "Work expands to fill the time available for its completion." This is not a cynical observation about laziness. It is a structural observation about human cognition: without a time constraint, work has no natural termination condition. The carousel was touched repeatedly for 13 days not because it needed 13 days of work, but because 13 days were available and no time constraint existed.

Time visibility does not add pressure. It adds information. You may decide that the information means you need to work faster, or that it means you need to cut scope, or that your pace is fine. But you cannot make any of those decisions without the information. Working without time visibility is navigating without instruments.

---

### Sub-Skill T2.1: Session Time-Boxing

A time-box is a fixed time limit for a specific activity. It is set before the activity begins. When the time-box ends, the activity ends — regardless of completion state.

**The basic time-box structure for a sang-logium build session:**

```
SESSION TIME-BOX: 90 minutes per component build
BUG-FIXING TIME-BOX: 15 minutes per CRITICAL bug
REVIEW TIME-BOX: 20 minutes per weekly velocity review
LOOP RECOGNITION TIME-BOX: 5 minutes — if I notice I am improving
                             a DoD-complete component, 5 minutes maximum
                             before forcing a move to the next component
```

The 90-minute component time-box is derived from research on focused work: cognitive performance on complex tasks degrades meaningfully after 90 minutes without a break. Separately, a component that requires more than 90 minutes is either scoped too broadly (return to Theme 1 of the Seven-Theme Curriculum) or has a CRITICAL bug blocking it (apply Debug Triage, Theme 6 of the Seven-Theme Curriculum).

**Setting a time-box:** Use a physical or phone timer. Not a mental estimate of when 90 minutes will have passed. The timer is the instrument. Your felt sense of time is not.

**What happens when the time-box ends on an incomplete component:**
Do not extend the time-box silently. Make an explicit decision: "I am extending this box by 30 minutes because the specific blocking issue is [X]." Write it down. This forces the extension to be a conscious choice, not a drift.

**Violation consequence — sang-logium:**
The carousel work had no time-box. It was worked on whenever it seemed to need attention. Over 17 days, "whenever it seems to need attention" consumed an estimated 3-5 days of work. A 90-minute time-box per component, strictly enforced, would have produced a working carousel (possibly rougher) in one session and locked it.

### Sub-Skill T2.2: The Daily Time Audit

At the end of every working day, before closing the editor, spend 5 minutes answering three questions:

```
QUESTION 1 — WHAT DID I ACTUALLY BUILD TODAY?
List every component or sub-task that moved from one state to a more complete state.
Be specific. "Worked on the carousel" is not specific. "Carousel renders 6 products
at 1280px, DoD item 1 and 2 pass" is specific.

QUESTION 2 — HOW MANY HOURS DID I WORK TODAY?
Not how many hours were available. How many were spent on actual development work
(not reading, not planning, not debugging non-blocking bugs).

QUESTION 3 — IS MY RATE OF PROGRESS CONSISTENT WITH FINISHING ON TIME?
Take total components locked / total components needed. Divide by days elapsed.
This gives your daily completion rate. Multiply by remaining days. Does it reach
the total components needed?
```

**Applied example (sang-logium, end of day 3):**

```
QUESTION 1: ProductSpotlight2 renders with real data at 1280px. DoD items 1-3 pass.
            Featured carousel renders but has a minor bug on mobile (DoD item 4 fails).
            ProductSpotlight3 exists as a skeleton (Pass 1 only).
            Locked today: 0 components fully.

QUESTION 2: 5 hours of actual development (2 hours on carousel mobile bug, 2 hours
            on ProductSpotlight2, 1 hour on ProductSpotlight3 skeleton).

QUESTION 3: 0 components locked / 9 needed, after 3 days.
            Daily rate: 0 components/day. Required rate: 1.5 components/day.
            At current rate, components locked at end of 6 days: 0.
            CONCLUSION: I am not on track. Intervention required.
```

This analysis on day 3 would have triggered the Amber Alert. The same analysis on day 5 (when the developer actually noticed the problem) produces the same conclusion — but with far less time to act on it.

**The rule:** The daily time audit is not optional on any day where a deadline exists. Five minutes. Three questions. The output is either "on track" or "amber alert." If amber alert: go to Theme T4 (Scope-Time Calibration) or Theme T5 (Recovery Protocols) immediately.

### Sub-Skill T2.3: The Component Completion Clock

For every component in the build, record the time it actually took from start to DoD-complete. This builds a personal reference class (Theme T1.1) for future estimation.

**Simple tracking:**

```
| Component               | Started        | Locked         | Actual Hours |
|-------------------------|----------------|----------------|--------------|
| Hero                    | Day 1, 9am     | Day 1, 2pm     | ~5 hours     |
| Shelf                   | Day 1, 2pm     | Day 1, 3pm     | ~1 hour      |
| RedesignFeatured...     | Day 2, 9am     | ???            | ???          |
```

After 3-5 projects, the pattern in this table is your personal reference class. When estimating the next project, you look at this table before generating any estimate. You do not rely on what you hope will happen. You look at what actually happened.

**Violation consequence — sang-logium:**
No component timing was tracked. This means there is no data to improve the next estimate. The developer who finishes the sang-logium homepage without this table will produce the same quality of estimate for the next project — and will be wrong by the same factor.

## Theme T3: Velocity Monitoring

### The Mental Representation Being Built

> "Velocity is not how hard I am working. It is how many deliverables are crossing the finish line per day. I can work extremely hard and have zero velocity. I need to track the output, not the effort."

### What Velocity Monitoring Is

Velocity monitoring is the practice of measuring the rate at which deliverables move from incomplete to DoD-complete, and comparing that rate against the rate required to finish on time.

It is distinct from tracking how much work is being done. A developer can commit 20 times in a day, fix 5 bugs, refactor a component, and update the design system — and have zero velocity because no component crossed from incomplete to DoD-complete. This distinction is invisible without explicit tracking.

### First Principle: The Ninety-Ninety Rule

Tom Cargill's Ninety-Ninety Rule: "The first 90% of the code accounts for the first 90% of the development time. The remaining 10% of the code accounts for the other 90% of the development time."

This rule describes the velocity collapse that happens in the late stages of a build. The cause: the first 90% is structural work (visible, measurable, satisfying to complete). The last 10% is integration work, edge cases, and polish — invisible, frustrating, and much harder to estimate.

The sang-logium failure showed the Ninety-Ninety Rule at work: the skeletal structure and basic data integration (first 90%) were done relatively quickly. The carousel refinement, the design system work, the mobile edge cases (last 10%) consumed the remaining time and more.

Velocity monitoring catches this pattern early. When the daily completion rate drops (components take longer to close out than they did at the start of the sprint), the Ninety-Ninety Rule is activating. The response is to triage ruthlessly and cut anything that is not required for the DoD.

---

### Sub-Skill T3.1: The Required Daily Rate

At the start of any project with a deadline, calculate the required daily completion rate:

```
REQUIRED RATE = total deliverables / available days

Sang-logium: 9 components / 6 days = 1.5 components per day
```

This number — 1.5 components per day — is the speedometer setting. Every day, you check your actual rate against this number. If your actual rate is below 1.5, you are falling behind. If it is above 1.5, you have buffer.

**The daily check (30 seconds):**

```
Components locked so far: [N]
Days elapsed: [D]
Actual rate: [N/D]
Required rate: [1.5]
On track: [yes/no]
If no: days off-pace: [actual shortfall in component-days]
```

**Applied to sang-logium, day 3:**

```
Components locked so far: 0
Days elapsed: 3
Actual rate: 0/3 = 0 components/day
Required rate: 1.5
On track: NO
Days off-pace: 4.5 component-days deficit (should have locked 4.5 components, locked 0)
```

This is an Amber Alert on day 3. The response is Theme T5 (Recovery Protocols).

**Why components locked — not commits or bug fixes:**
Commits are work performed. Components locked are deliverables completed. The distinction is the same as the difference between a carpenter's hours worked and the number of cabinets completed. Hours worked is input. Cabinets completed is output. Velocity is always measured in output.

---

### Sub-Skill T3.2: The Velocity Trend

A single day's velocity number is a data point. Three or more days' velocity numbers reveal a trend. The trend is more important than any single day.

**Three trend patterns and their implications:**

```
STABLE VELOCITY: Same number of components locked per day.
→ Your estimate is calibrated. Project will finish as predicted.
→ Action: continue.

ACCELERATING VELOCITY: More components locked each successive day.
→ You are building momentum (common as unfamiliar stack becomes familiar).
→ Action: recalculate projected finish date. Update Amber Alert.

DECELERATING VELOCITY: Fewer components locked each successive day.
→ The Ninety-Ninety Rule is activating. Complexity is growing. OR
→ The perfectionism loop is consuming time. OR
→ A large component is blocking without producing DoD completions.
→ Action: immediately identify the cause. Apply Theme T5.
```

**The sang-logium pattern:** Skeletal structure was built quickly (days 1-2, high velocity). Then the carousel consumed days 2-17 with no DoD completions (velocity collapsed to near zero). The trend was: initial progress → sudden stop. That stop was visible by day 4 if velocity was being tracked. It was not tracked, so it was not visible until March 15.

---

### Sub-Skill T3.3: The Blocker Identification Protocol

When velocity is zero or near zero for more than one day, a blocker exists. A blocker is any factor preventing components from reaching DoD. Blockers must be identified and named before they can be addressed.

**The five blocker types:**

```
TYPE 1 — SCOPE BLOCKER: The component is larger than its scope contract allowed for.
  → Symptom: component is "almost done" for multiple days without locking
  → Response: rewrite the scope contract. Cut the overage.

TYPE 2 — TECHNICAL BLOCKER: A bug prevents a DoD item from passing.
  → Symptom: one specific DoD item has been failing for more than one day
  → Response: apply 15-minute debug protocol. If not resolved: structured defer,
              placeholder, move on.

TYPE 3 — PERFECTIONISM BLOCKER: Component passes DoD but work continues anyway.
  → Symptom: all DoD items pass but the component is not committed as locked
  → Response: immediately commit, add to locked table, open next component.

TYPE 4 — PRIORITY BLOCKER: Time is being spent on a non-current-DoD task.
  → Symptom: velocity commits are C/D/E category but no A-category commits exist
  → Response: apply Debug Triage. Defer all non-CRITICAL work immediately.

TYPE 5 — ESTIMATION BLOCKER: The scope was underestimated at the project level.
  → Symptom: velocity is consistent but the required rate is impossible to sustain
  → Response: this is a project-level crisis. Go to Theme T4 or T5.
```

**Violation consequence — sang-logium:**
The primary blocker was Type 3 (perfectionism — the carousel passed functional criteria but was not locked) compounded by Type 4 (priority — time spent on C/D/E commits while A-commits were absent). Neither was identified as a blocker because velocity was not being tracked.

## Theme T4: Scope-Time Calibration

### The Mental Representation Being Built

> "Scope and time are the same lever pulled from different ends. When time is fixed and scope is flexible, cutting scope is not failure — it is professional execution. Delivering 7 components well by the deadline is superior to delivering 0 components perfectly by day 40."

### What Scope-Time Calibration Is

Scope-Time Calibration is the skill of adjusting the scope of a deliverable when time signals indicate that the original scope cannot be completed within the available time. It requires the ability to triage which components are essential, which are important, and which are expendable — and to make that triage decision quickly, under pressure, without emotional attachment to the original scope.

This skill is practiced in professional software development under the name "scope management" or "MVP (Minimum Viable Product) thinking." It is systematically absent in solo developers who have not been through a professional sprint review where a product owner explicitly cuts scope to protect a deadline.

### First Principle: The Iron Triangle

The Iron Triangle (also called the Triple Constraint) is one of the oldest and most validated models in project management. It states that every project has three constraints:

```
SCOPE ←————→ TIME
      ↖      ↗
        QUALITY
```

You can fix any two of the three. The third must flex. If you fix time (the deadline is March 15, non-negotiable) and fix quality (components must be visually correct with real data), then scope must flex. If you try to hold all three fixed simultaneously, all three degrade — you miss the deadline, the quality drops, and you deliver less scope anyway, but in the worst possible way.

The skill is knowing **which** scope to cut, not whether to cut.

---

### Sub-Skill T4.1: The MVP Component Triage

When Amber Alert fires (Theme T1.4) or velocity monitoring shows a critical deficit (Theme T3.1), immediately apply the MVP triage to the component list:

```
TIER 1 — MUST SHIP: Components that define the page. Without these, the page
         doesn't exist or doesn't work. These are never cut.

TIER 2 — SHOULD SHIP: Components that significantly improve the page. Cutting
         them noticeably degrades the product but the page still functions.

TIER 3 — COULD SHIP: Components that are additive. Cutting them is invisible
         to most visitors. The page is complete without them.
```

**Applied to sang-logium homepage:**

```
TIER 1 — MUST SHIP (never cut):
  Hero                — defines the page above the fold
  IemsGallery         — primary product catalogue section, core value
  Dacs                — core product category section
  Accessories         — core product category section

TIER 2 — SHOULD SHIP (cut only if absolutely necessary):
  RedesignFeatured+Spotlight — important showcase, complex to build
  ProductSpotlight2          — adds depth, less critical than gallery
  ProductSpotlight3          — adds depth, less critical than gallery

TIER 3 — COULD SHIP (cut first under time pressure):
  NewestRelease              — additive, nice-to-have
  Shelf (layout wrapper)     — structural, but absence is workable
```

**The decision rule:** If velocity monitoring shows you cannot finish all 9 components by the deadline, cut Tier 3 first, Tier 2 second. Never cut Tier 1. Deliver a complete, working Tier 1 on time rather than an incomplete all-tier page late.

**When to make the cut decision:**
On the Amber Alert date (Theme T1.4), not on the deadline date. Cutting on the Amber Alert date gives you remaining time to build the kept scope well. Cutting on the deadline date is damage control, not management.

---

### Sub-Skill T4.2: The Feature-Within-Component Triage

When a specific component is the blocker and cannot be cut from scope, triage within the component:

```
TIER 1 — MUST HAVE: The component renders with real data and passes core DoD items.
TIER 2 — SHOULD HAVE: Additional styling, hover states, responsive edge cases.
TIER 3 — COULD HAVE: Animations, micro-interactions, legacy device support.
```

**Applied to sang-logium carousel:**

```
TIER 1 (must have): Slides products. Prev/next works. Renders at 1280px.
                    No console errors. Real Sanity data.
TIER 2 (should have): Mobile layout at 375px. Touch swipe.
TIER 3 (could have): Animation timing refinement. Dot math precision.
                     Legacy iPhone compatibility. Orientation detection.
```

The carousel consumed weeks largely in Tier 3 features before Tier 1 was locked. Under scope-time calibration, Tier 1 must be locked before Tier 2 begins. Tier 3 does not begin until the deadline is confirmed safe.

---

### Sub-Skill T4.3: The Scope Cut Communication

When scope is cut — even if you are a solo developer with no client — write it down:

```
SCOPE CUT LOG — [date]
Original scope: [what was planned]
Cut scope: [what is being removed]
Reason: [velocity data showing the cut is necessary]
Impact: [what the page loses by cutting this]
Future: [when/if this will be added back]
```

This serves two purposes: it makes the cut decision explicit (preventing unconscious scope restoration), and it creates a log that informs future estimates by showing what was achievable in the given time.

## Theme T5: Recovery Protocols

### The Mental Representation Being Built

> "Being behind is a state, not a verdict. There is a specific sequence of actions that converts 'behind' into either 'on track' or 'explicitly adjusted.' There is no version of 'behind' that justifies panic, demoralization, or continued work at the same pace and direction."

### What Recovery Protocols Are

A Recovery Protocol is a specific, ordered sequence of decisions and actions to be taken when velocity monitoring indicates that the deadline will be missed at the current pace. It is pre-planned — decided before the crisis, not during it.

Pre-planning matters because decisions made under deadline panic are reliably worse than decisions made under calm planning conditions. The human brain under threat narrows its solution space, produces more errors, and fixates on "working harder" (which, if the work is misdirected, makes things worse) rather than "working differently."

The Recovery Protocol is the plan you make when you are calm that you execute when you are not calm.

---

### Sub-Skill T5.1: The Five-Step Recovery Sequence

When velocity monitoring confirms you are behind (Theme T3.1), execute these five steps in order. Do not skip steps. Do not start step 4 before completing step 3.

**STEP 1 — STOP (5 minutes):**
Stop all development work. Close the code editor. Open a text file.
Write: "Today is [date]. I have [N] days remaining. I have locked [X] of [Y] components. My required rate was [R]. My actual rate is [A]. I am [X] component-days behind."
This converts the feeling of being behind into a number. Numbers are manageable. Feelings are not.

**STEP 2 — DIAGNOSE (10 minutes):**
Identify which of the five blocker types (Theme T3.3) is causing the velocity deficit. Write it down. Be specific. "I am behind because the carousel has been open for 8 days and is consuming all build time" is specific. "I haven't been focused enough" is not a diagnosis.

**STEP 3 — TRIAGE (10 minutes):**
Apply MVP Component Triage (Theme T4.1) to the remaining scope. Write the Tier 1/2/3 classification for every remaining component. Calculate: if I build only Tier 1 items, do I finish by the deadline? Write the answer.

**STEP 4 — DECIDE (5 minutes):**
Make exactly one of three decisions. Write it down.

```
DECISION A — CUT SCOPE: Remove all Tier 3 components from this sprint.
             Possibly remove Tier 2 components. Deliver Tier 1 on time.

DECISION B — EXTEND DEADLINE: Move the deadline by [specific number] of days.
             This is only valid if the deadline is movable. Be specific about
             the new date. Do not say "a few more days." Say "March 22."

DECISION C — SURGE: Commit to [specific number] of additional hours per day
             for [specific number] of remaining days to close the gap.
             Only valid if the surge is physically sustainable and the velocity
             deficit is closable. Calculate: does the surge actually close the gap?
             If not: do not surge. Choose A or B.
```

**STEP 5 — EXECUTE (immediately after Step 4):**
Implement the decision from Step 4. If Decision A: update the scope document, remove Tier 3 from the sprint tracking table, immediately start the highest-priority Tier 1 item not yet locked. If Decision B: update the deadline everywhere it is written. Set a new Amber Alert date. Return to normal pace. If Decision C: set a timer for the additional hours. Begin the highest-priority incomplete Tier 1 item.

**Violation consequence — sang-logium:**
On March 4, the developer recognized the gap but no Recovery Protocol existed. The response was demoralization ("it's crushing") rather than a structured 30-minute protocol producing a specific decision. Without the protocol, "being behind" had no exit path. With the protocol, "being behind" has a 30-minute diagnosis-to-decision process.

---

### Sub-Skill T5.2: The Emergency Scope Floor

The Scope Floor is the minimum deliverable that counts as "done enough to show." It is established before the sprint begins, not during the crisis.

**Format:**
```
SCOPE FLOOR: The minimum state of [project name] that represents a viable,
             shippable product worth showing.

Sang-logium Scope Floor: All Tier 1 components (Hero, IemsGallery, Dacs,
Accessories) rendering with real data at desktop and mobile, with no console
errors and no visual breakage. ProductSpotlights at basic rendering level.
Featured section functional. NewestRelease can be placeholder.
```

The Scope Floor is the answer to "if everything goes wrong and I have to ship tomorrow, what is the minimum that ships?" Knowing this in advance prevents the panic of "I have to do everything" from producing the paralysis of "I can't do everything so I'll do nothing."

---

### Sub-Skill T5.3: The Surge Calculation

Before committing to a surge (working extra hours to close a velocity gap), calculate whether the surge actually closes the gap:

```
SURGE CALCULATION:
Current velocity: [X] components/day
Required velocity: [Y] components/day
Gap: [Y - X] components/day
Remaining days: [D]
Remaining components: [N]

At current velocity, projected finish: [D * X] components
Gap to deadline: [N - (D * X)] components

Surge adds: [additional hours/day * component completion rate/hour]
= [Z] additional components over [D] days

Does current + surge close the gap?
(D * X) + Z ≥ N → [yes/no]
```

If the surge does not close the gap: do not surge. Surging on a gap too large to close produces exhaustion, lower code quality (increasing debugging time), and no deadline improvement. Cut scope instead.

**A specific example:**
```
Current velocity: 0.3 components/day
Required velocity: 1.5 components/day
Remaining days: 4
Remaining components: 6

At current velocity: 4 * 0.3 = 1.2 components → miss by 4.8 components.

Surge: +4 hours/day = 0.7 additional components/day over 4 days = 2.8 additional
Total with surge: 1.2 + 2.8 = 4.0 components. Still miss by 2.

Conclusion: surge insufficient to close gap. Cut 2 components (from Tier 3/2)
or extend deadline by 7 days (2 components / 0.3 velocity).
```

## Theme T6: Time Psychology

### The Mental Representation Being Built

> "My subjective experience of time during development is not accurate data. The feeling of being productive is not evidence of progress. The feeling of being almost done is not evidence of being close. The feeling that something will 'only take 20 minutes' is a cognitive artifact, not a time estimate."

### What Time Psychology Is

Time psychology is the skill of recognizing the specific cognitive biases that distort your experience of time and pace during development, and applying behavioral countermeasures that operate at the behavioral level — because the biases cannot be removed by knowing about them.

This is the most important theme and the least taught. Kahneman's research is explicit: knowing about the Planning Fallacy does not protect you from it. Reading this section will not protect you from it. The protection comes from the behavioral practices in the earlier themes — the external instruments (timers, tracking tables, daily audits) that provide data your cognitive system cannot generate accurately on its own.

### The Five Cognitive Biases of Developer Time

**Bias 1: The Planning Fallacy (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979)**
You underestimate task duration by imagining the best-case scenario. You do this even when you know you've been wrong before. The correction is Reference Class Forecasting (Theme T1).

**Bias 2: Optimism Bias**
You believe your project is less likely to experience problems than comparable projects. You believe you are slightly more capable than average. Research shows only 30% of people hold this view of themselves, but 80% of people report holding it — meaning most people who think they're above average are wrong. The correction is the Uncertainty Multiplier (Theme T1.2).

**Bias 3: The Sunk Cost Effect Applied to Time**
When you have spent 3 hours on a bug, the 3 hours already spent makes you more likely to spend a 4th hour — not because the 4th hour is more likely to solve it, but because of the psychological pull of "I've already invested this much." This is how 15-minute debug sessions become 4-hour debugging spirals. The correction is the 15-Minute Rule (Theme 6 of the Seven-Theme Curriculum, combined with time-boxing from Theme T2).

**Bias 4: The "Almost Done" Illusion**
Work that is 90% done consistently feels 90% done for 100% of its remaining time. The carousel was "almost working" from day 4 through day 17. "Almost done" is not a time estimate. It is a feeling. The Ninety-Ninety Rule describes this precisely. The correction is measuring DoD items remaining, not progress level.

**Bias 5: The Productivity Feeling as Progress Proxy**
Intense focus and rapid commits produce a strong feeling of productivity. This feeling is indistinguishable, from the inside, from actual delivery progress. The carousel commits were intense, frequent, and felt productive. They produced zero A-category commits. The correction is the daily time audit and velocity monitoring (Themes T2 and T3).

---

### Sub-Skill T6.1: The "Only Takes 20 Minutes" Red Flag

The phrase "this will only take 20 minutes" — spoken internally or aloud — is a reliable signal that the Planning Fallacy is active. It is almost never correct when applied to software tasks.

**The rule:** Whenever you think "this will only take [N] minutes," apply the Uncertainty Multiplier from Theme T1.2. If the task is familiar technology + familiar pattern: 1.25x. If anything is unfamiliar: 1.5x minimum.

"This carousel fix will only take 20 minutes" → 20 * 1.5 = 30 minutes minimum, 60 minutes realistic.

But more importantly: ask whether the task is in scope. "This carousel fix will only take 20 minutes" is irrelevant if the carousel has already passed its DoD. The 20 minutes is not a time estimate for a valid task — it is the rationalization for entering the perfectionism loop.

**The two-question test:**
1. Is this task required by a current DoD item? (From Seven-Theme Curriculum, Theme 5)
2. If yes: does the Uncertainty Multiplied estimate fit within the current time-box?

If the answer to question 1 is no: do not do the task, regardless of its estimated duration.

---

### Sub-Skill T6.2: Demoralization as a Productivity Destroyer

The sang-logium developer noted: "it's crushing" upon recognizing the schedule gap on March 4. This is documented and real. Demoralization following schedule slippage is a real productivity destroyer — research on team performance consistently shows morale drops of 20-40% following missed milestones.

Demoralization is not a character weakness. It is a predictable response to discovering a large gap between expectation and reality. It is worsened when:
- The gap is discovered late (no warning, sudden shock)
- No recovery path is visible (no Recovery Protocol exists)
- The gap feels like evidence of incompetence (attribution to self rather than to biased estimation)

**The correct attribution of the sang-logium gap:**
The gap was not evidence of incompetence. It was evidence of the Planning Fallacy (a universal human cognitive bias), applied to an underestimated scope, without velocity monitoring to catch it early. These are structural failures of practice, not failures of character.

**The behavioral response to demoralization:**
1. Execute the Recovery Protocol immediately (Theme T5.1). Structured action is the only effective counter to spiral thinking.
2. Reframe the failure accurately: "My estimate was wrong because I used the inside view. My velocity was invisible because I was not tracking it. These are correctable with specific practices."
3. Calculate the actual remaining time. Do not catastrophize the total gap. Focus on: "Given where I am today, what is achievable by [new realistic date]?"

**Violation consequence — sang-logium:**
Discovering the gap on March 4 with no Recovery Protocol produced demoralization that was then worked through rather than resolved. The work continued at the same pace in the same direction for 11 more days. A Recovery Protocol on March 4 would have produced a 30-minute structured response that either corrected the pace or explicitly adjusted the expectation. The demoralization was real, but its duration and its impact on continued work was made worse by the absence of a structured response.

---

### Sub-Skill T6.3: The "Done" Feeling vs. DoD Compliance

Developers report feeling "done" with a component or feature before the DoD items have all passed. This is related to the Optimism Bias — the brain predicts success before it has arrived.

The "done" feeling is not evidence of doneness. It is evidence that the component is far enough along that the brain can simulate completion. The simulation is pleasant. It is not accurate.

**The behavioral rule:** Never commit a component as locked based on the "done" feeling. Verify every DoD item in the live browser, one by one, before the commit. This takes 5-10 minutes. It prevents "done" feelings from bypassing the DoD checklist.

**The specific practice:**
```
BEFORE LOCKING ANY COMPONENT:
□ Open browser to localhost
□ Open DevTools console
□ Read each DoD item aloud
□ Verify each item visually in the browser
□ Check console for errors
□ Only after all checkboxes are physically ticked: commit as locked
```

This is not bureaucratic. It is the behavioral countermeasure for a specific cognitive bias. The bias generates false completion feelings. The checklist generates accurate completion data.

## Part 2: How the Time Themes Integrate with the Seven Skill Themes

The Time Curriculum and the Seven-Theme Skill Curriculum are not independent. They address the same failure from two angles:

**The Seven Themes address: why work goes in the wrong direction.**
**The Six Time Themes address: why work takes too long even when it goes in the right direction.**

In practice, both failures interact:

- **Scoping failure (Skill Theme 1) creates estimation failure (Time Theme T1)**: When scope is not written precisely, estimates are generated for an imagined scope that expands during the build. The estimate was not wrong for the written scope — it was wrong because no scope was written.

- **Sequencing failure (Skill Theme 2) destroys velocity (Time Theme T3)**: Going deep on one component before all components are in Pass 2 means some components are in Pass 3 while others are in Pass 1. This creates uneven progress that looks like velocity but produces no new DoD completions.

- **Architecture failure (Skill Theme 3) inflates task duration (Time Theme T1 and T2)**: Building a library-grade carousel when a product-grade one is needed means the task is 3-5x larger than estimated. The estimate was generated for a product-grade implementation. The Uncertainty Multiplier does not account for voluntary scope over-engineering — only for genuine unknowns.

- **DoD absence (Skill Theme 5) enables the perfectionism loop (Time Theme T6)**: The "almost done" illusion (Time Theme T6, Bias 4) and the perfectionism loop (Skill Theme 5) are the same failure described from two angles. Without DoD, the perfectionism loop has no stopping condition. The time consequence is uncapped.

- **Triage failure (Skill Theme 6) prevents velocity recovery (Time Theme T3 and T5)**: When non-CRITICAL bugs are debugged during a velocity crisis, the velocity deficit grows wider while the debugging produces no DoD completions. The Recovery Protocol (Time Theme T5) is most effective when combined with rigorous Debug Triage (Skill Theme 6) — because the surge must be directed at A-category work, not at debugging work that could be deferred.

If both curricula had been practiced before the sang-logium sprint:

**Day 0 (before starting):**
- Reference class estimate: 14-21 days, not 6. Deadline set to March 18-20.
- Amber Alert date: March 8. Condition: fewer than 5 components locked.
- Scope Floor written: Hero, IemsGallery, Dacs, Accessories as non-negotiable.

- Daily audit: 0 components locked. Required rate: 1.5/day. Actual: 0.

- 1-2 days for integration QA and mobile verification.

**Day 15-16:**

The disaster was not inevitable. It was the product of predictable, correctable, well-researched failures. This curriculum addresses every one of them.
