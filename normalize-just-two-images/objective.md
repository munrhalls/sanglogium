The problem, exactly:

Both cards sit inside identical picture frames — same size, same cream background. But the product photo inside each frame was scaled and cropped independently, with no shared rule for how big the product should appear relative to its frame.

The Final Audio earbuds are shown small, floating roughly in the middle of the frame, with a large and fairly even amount of empty space surrounding them on all sides — top, bottom, left, and right. The product reads as a minor detail inside a mostly-empty box.

The Sony image shows two objects together — the earbud case and a separate carrying pouch — sized and cropped so they occupy a distinctly larger share of the same frame, sitting closer to its edges with much less empty space left over. It reads as full and substantial.

Placed next to each other, one card looks sparse and the other looks dense, purely because of how each photo happened to be framed before it was dropped into the page — not because of anything about the frame, the layout, or the page design itself. There was no shared standard applied across the two.

What good, professional status looks like:

Every product photo — whether it shows one item alone or a bundle of two — is scaled and cropped so the product occupies roughly the same proportion of its frame as every other product photo on the page, with a similar, even margin of breathing room around it on all sides.

Looking at the Final Audio card and the Sony card side by side, neither one should catch the eye first for looking bigger, smaller, fuller, or emptier than the other. They should carry the same visual weight — the same sense of how much of the box is "product" versus "space" — and sit with the same general centering and framing.

The two cards should feel like they came from one consistent, deliberate photography standard, not like two photos that were each cropped on their own terms and placed next to each other by coincidence.
